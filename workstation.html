        Velocity race

        Air separates when it can rise faster than the water carries it down. Downflow grows with depth as the screen adds water, so separation has to happen high, above the top disc, before the bubbles reach the fast water near the intake. Discs stop the cascade driving bubbles deep, spread the flow and make small bubbles merge.

        How the workstation works

        Physics from standard references

        • Downflow profile. Flow entering above the intake comes down the annulus, V(z) = Q_above × share(z) ÷ (π/4 × (casing ID² − column OD²)). With “Evenly along the perforations” the share grows from zero at the top of the perforations to all of it at the intake (or the bottom of the screen). “All above the top disc” is the conservative case. A spinner log is the way to know.
        • Where separation happens. Gas can still escape to the surface only from the water above the top disc, so the regime is judged at the top disc. At the intake the downflow is fastest; air carried that deep goes to the pump.
        • Small-bubble rise, default 0.6 ft/s: about 2–3 mm bubbles in real (contaminated) well water (Clift, Grace & Weber, Fig. 7.3). Plunging-jet bubbles are mostly 0.5–3 mm (Chanson), and they shrink with depth.
        • Merged-gas rise. The smaller of the annular slug formula U = (0.32 + 0.016 D_col/D_cas) √(g D_cas) (Kelessidis & Dukler 1989; D_col column OD, D_cas casing ID) and the large-bubble drift limit for large channels (Kataoka & Ishii 1987, at the top-disc pressure), times your safety factor.
        • Core velocity Vc = C0 × V. Bubbles ride the faster core of the flow (drift-flux distribution coefficient, default 1.2). Every regime check uses Vc.
        • Gas back through a disc. Counter-current flooding through the holes (Wallis 1969): with H = j ÷ √(g d) for the water velocity j through the holes and hole size d, gas cannot rise against the water once √H passes about 0.7–1.0. In practice that is always true through a perforated disc, so gas under a disc collects there unless a vent tube lets it out.
        • Disc head loss, perforated plate of thickness l (Idelchik, Diagram 8-3): ζ = [0.5(1−φ)^0.75 + τ(l/d)(1−φ)^1.375 + (1−φ)² + λ l/d] ÷ φ² on the local downflow velocity. φ counts holes, ports, the casing gap, the bore gap and the joint slots. Each disc is evaluated at its own depth.
        • Rubber sag. Annular plate clamped at the hub ring and free at the rim under the pressure across the deepest disc (Timoshenko & Woinowsky-Krieger), modulus from Shore A hardness (Gent) unless you enter one, stiffness reduced by the ligament efficiency of the hole pattern. It is linear theory: past about one thickness of droop the real sag is smaller, but the disc is coning. Confirm with a load test on one segment.
        • NPSH available: atmospheric head at site elevation + submergence − vapor pressure − disc losses. Intake and screen losses are not included, and entrained or dissolved gas raises the NPSH a pump really needs.
        • Flow change: stack head loss ÷ (pump curve slope + system curve slope). The system slope defaults to the drawdown per 100 gpm, because less flow also means less drawdown.

        Disc construction modeled

        • Water-jet-cut, potable-water-grade rubber segments run from the column bore to the casing. A split steel hub ring clamps over them, bolts through each segment and is tack-welded to the column.
        • Pass-through ports straddle the hub edge and the hub is notched around them. Holes keep clear of the hub, ports, joints and the outer edge by the edge margin.
        • The hub splits at two segment joints, so no bolt sits on a split.
        • Fabrication rules of thumb for rubber: holes and the rubber between them at least 0.6 × the rubber thickness.
        • Not modeled: fastener sizes beyond the bolt hole, coupling clearance, centralizers, vent tube routing, and the reverse load at shutdown without a check valve. Detail those with the fabricator.

        Analysis tools

        • Optimizer: exhaustive search over standard hole sizes and pitches under hard engineering constraints, a weighted ranking, and the trade-off (Pareto) frontier between flow loss and cutting length.
        • Monte Carlo risk: thousands of runs centred on the design pumping level. Flow, share of flow from above, bubble rise, pump curve slope, C0 (±0.1) and the disc loss coefficient (±20%) are drawn from triangular distributions; each run's pumping level follows its flow (drawdown scales with flow) plus your spread. Seeded, so the same inputs always give the same answer.
        • Sensitivity: one input at a time across the same ranges.

        Field rules to calibrate

        • Disc count, falling water: one disc per “fall or wetted screen per disc” of exposed perforation between the static level (or the top of the perforations, if deeper) and the design pumping level, plus one coalescing disc when the core velocity at the top disc beats small-bubble rise (minimum two).
        • Disc count, aquifer air: one disc per the same length of wetted screen above the intake; minimum one, or two when the core velocity beats small-bubble rise. Both sources: the larger count.
        • Placement: falling water starts below the design pumping level at your spacing; aquifer air builds up from the intake clearance at your spacing; both spreads the discs evenly over the zone, never closer than your spacing and never more than twice it.
        • Head-loss window per disc: enough to break the cascade's momentum, not so much it costs flow.

        What it does not know

        • Real bubble sizes, the gas fraction, how much of the casing wall the cascade covers, and the real inflow profile (a spinner log settles it).
        • Whether aquifer air arrives as free gas or as dissolved gas. Dissolved gas comes out as microbubbles that no disc can separate; measure total dissolved gas.
        • How gas pockets under discs behave without vent tubes. Watch motor amps and the discharge for air after install.
        • Casing condition: out-of-round, weld beads, scale or a liner change the usable disc OD. Run a drift check first.
        • The field rules (feet per disc, head-loss window) are judgment values. Calibrate them against wells with before-and-after results.
